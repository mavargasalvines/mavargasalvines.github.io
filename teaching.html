Teaching Philosophy

I strongly believe that learning is key to making better choices in every aspect of life, not only in academics. Experience has also taught me that teaching is the most efficient way to learn: explaining an idea to others reinforces what I already know and exposes what I still need to understand. As an economics educator, my goal is to develop my students’ ability to form their own well-reasoned opinions about the economy and the world around them, even when the information available to them is limited. I also aim to teach economics as a social science: one that seeks to reflect reality, simplifies it through mathematical models, and tests those models against empirical evidence.

I pursue this goal through active learning built on evidence about how students learn. In the courses that I teach, students complete spaced retrieval-practice homework, work through problems with their peers in peer-instruction review sessions that I hold (out-of-class), and are evaluated with a transparent participation rubric. Mastery-based exam-replacement policies reward improvement and give students a real reason to return to the material they missed.

I also treat my own classroom as a source of data. Item-level psychometric analysis of my assessments tells me which questions work, where students struggle, and which teaching practices to keep or revise, so the course improves every semester.

Finally, I consistently integrate real-life examples to bridge theory and practice, from inflation and trade shocks to the effects of generative AI on the economy. This ensures that students not only understand economic concepts but can also apply them to real-world problems. Teaching in two countries and two languages, at Virginia Tech and the Universidad de Piura, has taught me to adapt these tools to very different students while keeping the same goal.
